STUDENT LAB MODULE Biomechanical Analysis & ACL Prevention

Jump-Landing Biomechanics & Injury Risk Lab Questions

Review the questions below based on the OpenLESS data, Padua et al. (2009), and Turner et al. (2024). Click each question to reveal detailed biomechanical explanations.

1

Why does a knee flexion angle < 30° at initial ground contact significantly increase ACL injury risk?

Focus on quadriceps tendon angle of insertion and anterior tibial shear force.

Biomechanical Mechanism: When the knee lands in extended or near-extended alignment (< 30° flexion), the patellar tendon attaches to the tibial tuberosity at an acute angle that pulls the proximal tibia primarily anteriorly rather than compressing the joint. When the quadriceps vigorously contract to decelerate the body, this anterior pull creates massive anterior tibial shear force. Because the Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) is the primary anatomical restraint against anterior tibial translation, this shear force directly loads the ligament. In contrast, at flexion angles > 30° (as observed in Trial 1: ~36°), the patellar tendon vector is oriented more along the longitudinal axis of the tibia, producing protective joint compression while simultaneously recruiting hamstring co-activation to pull the tibia posteriorly.
2

How does proactive forward trunk lean alter external joint moment arms and reduce knee loading?

Compare the center of mass (COM) position in Stepoff-Leaning vs. Stepoff-Upright.

Biomechanical Mechanism: Forward trunk flexion (45.2° in Trial 1) shifts the body's center of mass (COM) anteriorly, positioning the Ground Reaction Force (GRF) vector closer to the knee joint center and further from the hip joint center. This reduces the external knee flexion moment arm and increases the external hip flexion moment arm. Consequently, the work of decelerating downward momentum is transferred away from the anterior quadriceps/knee complex and into the larger, more powerful posterior chain musculature (gluteus maximus, hamstrings, and spinal extensors). In contrast, in Trial 2 (upright spine, -6.3°), the GRF vector passes far posterior to the knee, creating an enormous external knee flexion moment that requires maximum quadriceps tension, exacerbating anterior tibial shear.
3

What constitutes "dynamic knee valgus" in 3D biomechanics, and how does OpenLESS identify it?

Examine Criteria 8, 9, 10, 16, and 17 (hip adduction, hip internal rotation, tibial abduction).

Biomechanical Mechanism: True dynamic valgus is a tri-planar collapse consisting of femoral adduction, femoral internal rotation, tibial external rotation/abduction, and foot pronation (eversion). In OpenCap's inverse kinematics model, OpenLESS captures this primarily through hip adduction and hip internal rotation during stance. In Trial 2 (Upright), the subject demonstrated abnormal hip adduction (> 1°) and internal rotation (> 8°) along with a massive 10.8° lateral hip shift at peak depth (Item 16 & 17 error). This inward collapse creates a valgus moment arm at the knee that subjects the ACL to combined tension and impingement against the intercondylar notch.
4

How does the impulse-momentum theorem explain the difference between a "soft" and "stiff" landing?

Examine the time duration of stance and joint angular displacements.

Biomechanical Mechanism: The impulse-momentum relationship dictates that the change in momentum ($\Delta p = m \cdot \Delta v$) is equal to the integral of net force over time ($\int F \, dt$). In both trials, the subject falls from the same height and must arrest the same vertical momentum to come to a stop ($\Delta p$ is constant). In Trial 1 (Soft Landing), the deep multi-joint flexion (45° knee displacement, 39° hip displacement) lengthens the deceleration time ($\Delta t$). Because $\Delta t$ is large, the peak ground reaction force ($F_{peak}$) is low. In Trial 2 (Stiff Landing), the joints undergo minimal displacement (only 19-23° knee flexion), halting downward velocity in a fraction of the time. Consequently, the ground reaction force spike is extraordinarily high, delivering jarring impact shocks to cartilage, subchondral bone, and passive ligaments.
